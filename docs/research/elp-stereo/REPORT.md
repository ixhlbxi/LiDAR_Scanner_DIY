# ELP stereo pair sharpens bearings, not ranges

The two ELP 16 MP IMX298 cameras suit two of the three jobs planned for them. They are strong for **target bearings**: a 0.2 px corner error is about 0.008° with the stock lens, about 2 mm at 15 m. They are adequate for **point-cloud colorization**, provided each LiDAR point is projected into each calibrated camera, not onto a PiLiDAR-style panorama. They are weak for **metric stereo range**. Beyond about 5–8 m, stereo is a qualitative cross-check at best, and it depends on the two cameras holding their relative yaw to about 0.008°. The biggest correction to the design conversation is not about the cameras. It is that the LD19 is specified for **0.02–12 m**. That means **no LiDAR range to targets at 15–30 m**, so the "camera bearing plus LiDAR range" resection only works for targets inside about 10 m ([Waveshare LD19 wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/DTOF_LIDAR_LD19)). Beyond that you need bearings-only resection to 3–4 targets, or an RTK/PPK position with the targets supplying heading only. The camera facts that matter are these. The real focal length is unknown anywhere from **~1,400 to ~2,500 px**. Distortion is unpublished despite the "no distortion" label. Full-resolution YUY2 is physically marginal over USB 2.0 isochronous transfer. Two full-resolution streams cannot run at once on the Pi 4's single shared USB 2.0 bus. None of this blocks the plan, but several numbers from the conversation need re-scoping. The claim-by-claim table below says which ones. Research date: 2026-09-29.

## The camera is probably an ELP-USB16MP01-BH120 with an unmeasured lens

The housing, lens and sensor match ELP's **ELP-USB16MP01-BH120**: the IMX298 board with the "H120" no-distortion M12 lens in a black box housing, with a 3 m standard cable. The bare-board version is the H120. ELP's own page says the lens has an **"FOV about 118 degree"**. It never says whether that angle is horizontal, vertical or diagonal, and publishes no focal length, f-number or distortion figure ([ELP H120](https://www.elpcctv.com/16-megapixel-120-degree-no-distortion-hd-usb-micro-camera-module-wide-angle-p-361.html); [ELP BH120](http://www.elpcctv.com/oem-elp-16mp-full-hd-1080p-no-distortion-mini-usb-camera-wide-angle-free-driver-p-369.html)). Three checks will confirm the model: the label on the housing or box, the `iProduct` string in `lsusb -v`, and the cable length. An eBay listing of the same product carries UPC 757350433780, which gives a fourth check ([eBay](https://www.ebay.com/itm/388476557016)).

The sensor facts are solid. The Sony IMX298 is a 2015–16-era stacked BSI smartphone sensor with a **4656×3496 active array of 1.12 µm pixels**, which makes a 5.215 × 3.916 mm image area and a 6.521 mm diagonal. It has a Bayer filter, a rolling shutter and on-chip PDAF. Sony says it is designed only for phones and tablets ([Sony IMX298 product brief](https://dlscorp.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/ProductBrief_IMX298_20160210.pdf)). The USB bridge hides almost all of that. You get ISP-processed MJPEG or YUY2, never RAW. PDAF is unused. The sensor's hardware dual-sync feature is not exposed over USB, so the two units cannot be hardware-synchronized. Several listing figures are wrong. ELP's "Image Area 6.4 × 5.3 mm" is close to Sony's *die* size (6.433 × 4.921 mm), not the active array. Amazon's "20 mm–200 m focal length" is template filler. The f/1.8 appears on no ELP page. ELP's own two websites disagree about whether 1280×960 MJPEG runs at 10 or 30 fps ([webcamerausb H120](https://www.webcamerausb.com/elp-free-driver-16mp-webcam-plug-and-play-cmos-imx298-sensor-micro-distortion-120-degree-wide-angle-uhd-usb-camera-module-for-ocr-document-scanning-p-408.html)).

The focal length is the number the design depends on most, and it is genuinely unknown. On this sensor, "118°" gives three very different answers depending on the projection:

| Interpretation | Centre focal length (full res) | Implied VFOV | 20 cm board at 15 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 118° horizontal, rectilinear | 1,399 px (1.57 mm) | 102.7° | 18.7 px |
| 118° diagonal, rectilinear | 1,749 px (1.96 mm) | 90.0° | 23.3 px |
| 118° horizontal, equidistant (f-θ) | ~2,261 px | ~88° | ~30 px |
| "2.8 mm" per one ELP/Amazon listing | 2,500 px | — | ~33 px |

The rows come from the calculations in the research notes, using [Sony's array size](https://dlscorp.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/ProductBrief_IMX298_20160210.pdf) and the [Amazon 118° listing](https://www.amazon.com/ELP-Raspberry-118degree-Distortion-Industrial/dp/B0C289GYVZ). A rectilinear 2.8 mm lens on this sensor would give only 86° HFOV, so "118°" and "2.8 mm" cannot both be true of an undistorted lens. A comparable Arducam IMX298 board shows the same kind of mismatch. Arducam quotes 105° H with a 2.72 mm EFL, while a rectilinear 2.72 mm lens would give 87.6°. That gap implies strong barrel compression ([Arducam B0268 datasheet](https://www.uctronics.com/download/Amazon/B0268_16MP_Wide_Angle_UVC_Camera_Datasheet.pdf)). Treat "no distortion" as marketing. Calibrate with OpenCV's rational model and with `cv2.fisheye` (Kannala-Brandt), and compare the two on held-out images. The pinhole-rational family is generally usable to about 120°, and 118° sits right at that boundary ([CalibrX](https://calibrx.io/blog/pinhole-wide-fisheye-camera-models/)). The calibrated fx settles the question: ~1,750 px means a near-rectilinear lens with a diagonal FOV, and ~2,260 px means it is closer to equidistant.

The lens is a changeable **M12 (S-mount)** ([webcamerausb H120](https://www.webcamerausb.com/elp-free-driver-16mp-webcam-plug-and-play-cmos-imx298-sensor-micro-distortion-120-degree-wide-angle-uhd-usb-camera-module-for-ocr-document-scanning-p-408.html)). On the analogous Arducam board, the IR-cut filter sits inside the lens barrel, so any replacement lens must include its own IR-cut filter or outdoor colours will come out magenta ([Arducam B0268](https://www.uctronics.com/download/Amazon/B0268_16MP_Wide_Angle_UVC_Camera_Datasheet.pdf)). Low-distortion 6 mm and 8 mm M12 lenses sized for 1/1.8" cost about **US $19** with a 650 nm IR-cut option ([Commonlands CIL062](https://commonlands.com/products/no-distortion-6mm-m12-lens); [CIL083](https://commonlands.com/products/low-distortion-8mm-m12-lenses)). They are rated for 6–8 MP, so they will not fully resolve 1.12 µm pixels. A 6 mm lens gives about 5,357 px focal length but only 47° × 36° field of view.

## One shared 480 Mbit/s bus forces one camera at a time

The Pi 4's four ports all hang off one VIA VL805 controller, and Raspberry Pi documents that "the USB 2.0 lines on all four ports are connected to a single USB 2.0 hub within the VL805". As a result, **both cameras, the F9P, the LD19's CP2102 and the ESP32 share one 480 Mbit/s bus**, whichever port each is plugged into. The same page caps USB current at **1,200 mA total across all ports** ([Raspberry Pi docs](https://github.com/raspberrypi/documentation/blob/master/documentation/asciidoc/computers/raspberry-pi/usb-bus-on-raspberry-pi.adoc)). Using a blue USB 3 port or a USB 3 hub does not add USB 2.0 bandwidth. A USB 3 SSD, on the other hand, uses the separate SuperSpeed path and belongs there.

The failure mechanism is in the kernel. `uvcvideo` reserves isochronous bandwidth at STREAMON, based on the payload size the camera requests. For compressed formats such as MJPEG, the `UVC_QUIRK_FIX_BANDWIDTH` (quirks=128) recalculation is skipped entirely ([uvc_video.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/torvalds/linux/master/drivers/media/usb/uvc/uvc_video.c)). One high-bandwidth isochronous endpoint moves at most 3 × 1,024 B per 125 µs microframe, about **24.6 MB/s**. A full-resolution MJPEG frame is likely 2–3 MB (an estimate; ELP does not publish it), so 10 fps needs 20–30 MB/s. A camera sized for that mode will almost certainly request the largest alternate setting, and the second camera then gets ENOSPC ("No space left on device"). This pattern is documented on the Pi 4 itself: two webcams at only 800×448 YUYV hang with "USB isochronous frame lost (-18)", and quirks=128 did not help ([raspberrypi/linux #3406](https://github.com/raspberrypi/linux/issues/3406)). OpenPNP users saw the same with two identical ELP HD cameras ([OpenPNP group](https://groups.google.com/g/openpnp/c/_WpAoqkPLl4)). Because the reservation is made at STREAMON rather than at open(), both nodes can stay open and configured. **Only streaming has to be serialized**: STREAMON A, grab, STREAMOFF A, then the same for B. The reservation also squeezes the other devices. A Pi 4 running two 720p cameras caused Klipper USB-CAN timeouts ([crowsnest #202](https://github.com/mainsail-crew/crowsnest/issues/202)). The worst case here is LD19 or F9P serial gaps while a camera streams, and that is why the serial-gap test below matters.

**Full-resolution YUY2 is physically marginal.** A 4656×3496 YUY2 frame is 32.55 MB, which needs at least 1.32 s over a single isochronous endpoint. ELP's "1 fps" is therefore only possible if the bridge uses **bulk** transfers ([ELP frame-rate table](https://www.elpcctv.com/16-megapixel-120-degree-no-distortion-hd-usb-micro-camera-module-wide-angle-p-361.html)). The repo's `docs/HARDWARE.md` repeats the 1 fps figure; flag it as unverified. One `lsusb -v` line settles it, and the answer decides a lot: with bulk endpoints two cameras share bandwidth and just run slower, while with isochronous endpoints the second one fails outright. The reason YUY2 matters at all is calibration quality. A MathWorks engineer's guidance is not to calibrate from JPEGs, because compression artefacts bias corner detection ([MATLAB Answers](https://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/answers/163015-reason-for-checkerboard-corner-detection-to-fail)). The practical split is lossless Y-plane captures for the rare calibration sessions and MJPEG for routine scans. If full-res YUY2 turns out unusable, the fallback is YUY2 at 2320×1744. Intrinsics are mode-specific, though, so that only works if you also scan in that mode.

The remaining integration facts are straightforward. Picamera2/libcamera is built for CSI cameras. A Raspberry Pi engineer says its apps "can't produce the YUV420 images" they request from UVC webcams, and MJPEG recording fails in Picamera2 ([RPi forum](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=347113); [picamera2 #788](https://github.com/raspberrypi/picamera2/issues/788)). The repo's `src/rover/camera.py` is a Picamera2 HQ-camera module and will need a V4L2 path. For stills, use plain V4L2 or OpenCV `CAP_V4L2`, set the MJPG FOURCC before width and height, and **write the MJPEG bytes to disk undecoded**. No Pi 4 benchmark for decoding a 16 MP JPEG was found; the estimate is several hundred ms. A decoded BGR frame is 48.8 MB. Identical ELP units report the same serial, so **udev must key on port path** (`ID_PATH`, `KERNELS=="1-1.x"`, `ATTR{index}=="0"`), and the cables need labels ([RPi forum](https://forums.raspberrypi.com/viewtopic.php?t=145943)). Each camera draws 150–240 mA ([ELP BH120](http://www.elpcctv.com/oem-elp-16mp-full-hd-1080p-no-distortion-mini-usb-camera-wide-angle-free-driver-p-369.html)). Two cameras plus the F9P, the ESP32 and an SSD can exceed the 1.2 A budget, so use a powered hub, which fits DEC-018. The rolling shutter does not matter on a stopped platform, but it does while the mast is still ringing after a stop. Gate capture on the MPU-9250 gyro being quiet, not on a fixed delay.

## Bearings are excellent; the LD19's 12 m range is the real limit

On the camera side the targeting geometry works. A 20 cm board at 15 m subtends 0.76°, which is **18.7–23.3 px wide with the stock lens if it is rectilinear, about 30 px if it is equidistant, and 71 px with a 6 mm lens**. Wherever the target lands, bearing precision is not the constraint. A 0.2 px corner error is about 2 mm of lateral position at 15 m. The constraint is the number of pixels available for *detection*. AprilTag guidance recommends 5 px per bit ([Optitag](https://optitag.io/blogs/news/designing-your-perfect-apriltag)). By that rule a tag36h11 needs to be about **54 cm** at 15 m with the stock lens. A 2×2 "X" saddle target with 20 px quadrants needs about **43 cm at 15 m and 86 cm at 30 m**. Small multi-square checkerboards fail at these ranges: a 4×4 board 20 cm across gets 5–6 px squares, and `findChessboardCorners` will not find them reliably. That fits LightBurn users' "Pattern NOT found" reports with ELP 16 MP cameras ([LightBurn forum](https://forum.lightburnsoftware.com/t/trouble-calibrating-elp-16mp-camera/120980)).

A flat target on the ground is worse still. From a 1.5 m mast, a target 10 m away is seen at atan(1.5/10) ≈ **8.5° grazing**, so a 20 cm plate is foreshortened to 20 cm × sin 8.5° ≈ **3 cm of apparent height**. With the stock lens that is **4–5 px tall**, which is hopeless for detection even though its width is 28–35 px. Targets must stand **vertically on rods**, facing the scanner.

The eccentricity argument against bull's-eyes is true in principle but immaterial at this scale. Luhmann shows that the centre of the imaged ellipse is not the projected centre of the circle ([Luhmann 2014](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XL-5/363/2014/)). For a 20 cm circle the bias works out to about R²·sin t·cos t / Z. That is **about 0.5 mm at 10 m** in the worst case (45° tilt), or 0.02–0.07 px with the stock lens, one to two orders of magnitude below LD19 range noise. Checker/X targets are still the right choice, for a different reason: the saddle point stays well defined under blur, defocus and JPEG ringing, and does not depend on a threshold level.

The real problem is on the LiDAR side. The LD19 datasheet gives **0.02–12 m range, ±45 mm accuracy, 10 mm σ, 30 klx ambient-light tolerance and a ±2° angle error** ([Botland LD19 datasheet mirror](https://botland.de/img/art/inne/21991_Karta%20katalogowa.pdf); [Waveshare](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/DTOF_LIDAR_LD19)). At 0.8° per point, a 20 cm target at 10 m gets **1–2 hits per scan line**, and those are likely mixed edge returns. Range-plus-bearing resection is therefore a **≤10 m technique** with the current LiDAR. Within that range it is sound. With the scanner levelled, the unknowns are E, N and heading. Two targets give four observations for three unknowns. With ranges σ = 1–4.5 cm crossing at 90°, position error is about **1–4.5 cm**. Heading error for targets 10 m apart is about **0.08–0.24°**. A 0.1° heading error puts 1.7 cm of lateral error on a point at 10 m, which is significant against the ±5–10 cm system goal. To lift range precision above a single return, use a **40–60 cm flat plate** behind the X, fit a plane to the many LiDAR points on it, and intersect the camera's bearing ray with that plane. Past 12 m there are two options. One is bearings-only resection, which needs three targets and fails when the station sits on the circle through all three (the Snellius-Pothenot danger circle); a fourth target adds redundancy. The other is to let GNSS supply position so the targets only fix heading. With RTK at σ = 2 cm and one target at 10 m, heading is good to about 0.11°. The F9P results are encouraging for that route. Twenty-minute static F9P sessions post-processed in RTKLIB demo5 gave **sub-decimetre median horizontal error even under full mixed canopy** ([Tomaštík & Everett, Sensors 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10056071/)).

## Stereo depth is a near-field cross-check that depends on a 0.008° yaw tolerance

The stereo arithmetic from the conversation is correct. With ΔZ = Z²·Δd/(f·B), B = 0.165 m and Δd = 0.2 px, the stock lens gives **1.7–2.2 cm at 5 m and 15.6–19.5 cm at 15 m**, and a 6 mm lens gives 0.6 cm and 5.1 cm. The weak point is the 0.2 px assumption. It is realistic for well-resolved checker corners: per-image σ ≈ 0.1 px gives disparity σ ≈ 0.14 px. The best published long-range stereo result is a "consistent error limit of 1/10 pixel", and that came from an object-level, carefully optimized pipeline ([Pinggera et al., ECCV 2014](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-10605-2_7)). At 15 m, though, a 20 cm target is only about 20 px wide, so budget **0.3–0.5 px**, which means 30–50 cm of depth error. Dense SGBM on natural texture typically achieves 0.3–1 px because of pixel-locking bias in sub-pixel interpolation ([sub-pixel SGM](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/292150125_New_Sub-Pixel_Interpolation_Functions_for_Accurate_Real-Time_Stereo-Matching_Algorithms)). That is roughly 0.3–1 m at 15 m with the stock lens.

Matching noise is not the largest error, though. **Mechanical drift is.** A relative yaw error δ between the cameras shifts every disparity by f·δ px. That error is systematic and does not average out. Keeping it under 0.2 px requires δ < **0.008°** with the stock lens and **0.002°** with a 6 mm lens. An M12 barrel creeping in its thread, a flexing cheese plate or a temperature change can each exceed that. The baseline length also matters more than the conversation implied. Depth scales linearly with B, so a 1 mm error on 165 mm is a **0.6% scale error: 3 cm at 5 m and 9 cm at 15 m**. The conclusion that you don't need a tape measure is right, but for a stronger reason: a tape is not accurate enough. `stereoCalibrate` recovers B with the scale of the calibration board, so it is only as good as the calipered square size of that board. Calibrate each camera's intrinsics separately with a ChArUco board, 25+ images, full-frame coverage and tilts up to about 45°, aiming for 0.2–0.5 px RMS ([Limelight ChArUco docs](https://docs.limelightvision.io/docs/docs-limelight/getting-started/performing-charuco-camera-calibration); [calib.io](https://calib.io/blogs/knowledge-base/5-biggest-calibration-mistakes)). Then run `stereoCalibrate` with `CALIB_FIX_INTRINSIC` on a static board. Unsynchronized USB cameras are fine for that as long as nothing moves.

The conversation also said a camera rotating in place has no baseline. That is true about the camera's own centre, where images relate by the depth-free homography K·R·K⁻¹. It is only partly true here. Cameras mounted 80–90 mm off the mast axis sweep a chord baseline of b = 2r·sin(Δψ/2): **about 90 mm for 60° of rotation, and up to about 140 mm** while a point stays within the 118° view. That parallax depends on knowing the stepper angle, since 0.05° of step error is about 1.2 px. Its best use is recovering the camera-to-axis offset in a per-station bundle adjustment, not depth.

## Colorization needs per-camera projection, and no DIY build has done it

No DIY project turned up that pairs two ELP 16 MP cameras or runs stereo on a rotating 2D-LiDAR scanner. PiLiDAR, this repo's inspiration, uses **one HQ camera with an M12 fisheye**. It shoots 4 (to 16) photos, stitches them with a fixed Hugin template, and colours each point from the pixel at that point's longitude and latitude **seen from the scan origin**. That ignores the offset between camera and LiDAR. It has no camera-to-LiDAR extrinsic calibration and publishes no accuracy figure ([PiLiDAR README](https://github.com/PiLiDAR/PiLiDAR); [pointcloud.py](https://github.com/PiLiDAR/PiLiDAR/blob/main/lib/pointcloud.py)). Its LiDAR-to-axis offsets (−1.05°, −37.5 mm, −41.9 mm) are hand-tuned constants in `config.json` ([config.json](https://github.com/PiLiDAR/PiLiDAR/blob/main/config.json)). The repo is still maintained, with its last commit on 2026-05-08. With cameras about 82 mm off-axis, that nodal approximation mis-aims colour by about 2.3° at 2 m and 0.5° at 10 m. That is tens of pixels at 16 MP.

The other builds offer fewer lessons. The Scanse Sweep kit and Charles' Labs scanners have no camera. Their recurring faults were wobbly mechanics, uncalibrated sensor offsets that curve straight walls, and a black enclosure that got "very hot in direct sunlight" ([Servo Magazine](https://www.servomagazine.com/magazine/article/the-multi-rotor-hobbyist-scanse-sweep-3d-scanner-review); [Charles' Labs](https://charleslabs.fr/en/project-3D+Lidar+Scanner)). A 2025 low-cost TLS built on a Livox Avia and a Pi CM4 (€2,050) used **no camera and no GNSS**. It relied on total-station-surveyed markers plus ICP and NDT, and reached a DBH RMSE of 1.50 cm ([Výbošťok et al., Sensors 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12787914/)). Commercial scanners handle colour with multi-camera HDR: the Leica BLK360 uses four 13 MP cameras ([Leica](https://leica-geosystems.com/products/laser-scanners/scanners/leica-blk360)). Pi stereo projects use short baselines and low resolution. StereoPi uses 65 mm. The mpr-projects Pi 5 rig reached 0.27 px reprojection error with unsynchronized captures of static scenes, but quantified no depth accuracy ([mpr-projects](https://mpr-projects.com/index.php/2025/05/08/stereo-vision-with-two-rpi-cameras/)).

The pipeline therefore has to be written. For each image, the camera pose is R_z(ψ_i)·T_platform_cam. Project the points with `cv2.projectPoints` or `cv2.fisheye.projectPoints`. Build a z-buffer to reject points hidden from that camera. Pick the best view for each point by distance from the principal point, angle of incidence and sharpness. PDAL's `filters.colorization` cannot do this, because it samples a top-down raster by XY with no perspective or occlusion ([PDAL](https://pdal.io/en/stable/stages/filters.colorization.html)). The stereo pair's real value for colour is its second viewpoint 165 mm away. It fills points occluded in one camera, and disagreement between the two cameras flags occlusion or glare. For the camera-to-LiDAR extrinsic, two approaches fit a static dense per-station cloud. The targetless koide3 `direct_visual_lidar_calibration` supports fisheye lenses ([GitHub](https://github.com/koide3/direct_visual_lidar_calibration)). The other is a plane-based solve over 4–6 board poses in the style of Zhang and Pless ([Zhang & Pless 2004](https://openscholarship.wustl.edu/cse_research/1103/)). Using both gives a cross-check.

One repo conflict needs a decision. **DEC-026 ("Camera as Visual Reference, Not Photogrammetry")** and the Picamera2-based `camera.py` do not cover target resection or stereo. Using the ELPs for metric work supersedes DEC-026 and should be recorded as a new DEC.

## Next steps, hardware tests, target design and capture sequence

Run the tests in this order. The first two decide the capture architecture, and everything after them is tuning.

| Priority | Test | How | What it decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | USB transfer type | `lsusb -v -d <vid:pid>`: VideoStreaming endpoint "Transfer Type" (Isochronous/Bulk), `wMaxPacketSize` per alt setting; note `iProduct` | Bulk = dual streams possible and full-res YUY2 viable; iso = strict one-at-a-time; confirms BH120 |
| 2 | Formats and controls | `v4l2-ctl -d /dev/videoN --list-formats-ext` and `--list-ctrls-menus` | Real mode list and fps; whether `auto_exposure`, `exposure_time_absolute`, `white_balance_automatic`, `sharpness` and a JPEG quality control exist |
| 3 | Dual-stream test | Stream both at 4656×3496 MJPEG, then 1920×1080 MJPEG; watch `dmesg` for ENOSPC / `-18` | Confirms serialization need; whether reduced-mode concurrency works |
| 4 | Serial-gap test | Log LD19 (CP2102) and F9P read gaps and overruns with one camera streaming vs idle | Whether cameras may stream during LiDAR acquisition or only between sweeps |
| 5 | Full-res YUY2 timing | Grab 5 frames; time per frame | Calibration capture format (full-res Y plane vs 2320×1744 mode) |
| 6 | Intrinsics, rational vs fisheye | ChArUco on rigid dibond, 25+ frames, corners covered, sharpness at minimum; hold out 20% | True fx (settles 1,400 vs 2,260 px), distortion model, target px budget |
| 7 | Calibration repeatability | Recalibrate after power cycle, after 30 min warm-up, after transport; 50 static captures for corner σ | Corner σ (validates 0.2 px); intrinsic/extrinsic drift vs the 0.008° yaw tolerance |
| 8 | Settle and rolling-shutter check | After a stepper stop, log MPU-9250 gyro until quiet; image a straight edge | Gyro-gate threshold and minimum wait |
| 9 | Power | Everything on; `vcgencmd get_throttled`; `dmesg` for `-71`/`-110` | Powered-hub need (1.2 A cap) |
| 10 | LD19 on target plate | Scan a 50 cm matte plate at 3, 6, 9, 12 m; count points, fit plane | Real range limit and plane-fit σ for resection |

The recommended target is a **matte, rigid, vertically mounted 2×2 black-white "X" saddle target, 45–60 cm square**, on a plumb rod over the surveyed point. The flat plate itself doubles as the LiDAR plane. Add a coarse ArUco 4×4 marker beside it for ID, or identify targets from approximate azimuth. Use at least **three targets** so a bearings-only fallback exists. Keep two of them **within 10 m** and roughly **90° apart as seen from the station**, spaced as far apart as possible to reduce heading error. Beyond 15 m, scale the X to about 86 cm per 30 m of range with the stock lens, or fit the $19 6 mm lens to one camera and accept its narrow 47° view. If the targets also have to serve as UAV tie points, Urbančič's three-plane target with a black circle on top reached 0.7–1.0 cm RMSE, against 1.8–7.4 cm for spheres ([Urbančič 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6679335/)).

The capture sequence per station follows from the constraints above. Open both cameras once at startup through `/dev/cam_left` and `/dev/cam_right` symlinks keyed on port path. Apply manual exposure, gain and white balance, read them back, and repeat after every open. At each mast stop, ramp the stepper down, keep the A4988 enabled, and wait for the gyro norm to stay below about 0.05°/s for 100–200 ms (with a timeout). Then for the left camera: STREAMON, discard frames until their kernel timestamp follows the gate time plus about two buffers, keep one MJPEG buffer, STREAMOFF. Repeat for the right camera. Hand the bytes to a writer thread on the USB 3 SSD. The JSONL record should carry the camera ID, by-path, kernel timestamp, mast angle, gyro RMS and the control values actually read back. Expect **1–3 s per station** for both cameras, with no decoding on the Pi. Calibration sessions are separate, rare, and use lossless Y-plane captures. Record the change of camera role as a new decision superseding DEC-026, replace the Picamera2 path in `camera.py` with V4L2, and mark the "YUY2 full-res @ 1 fps" line in `docs/HARDWARE.md` as unverified until test 1 is run.

## Conclusion

The ELP pair is best understood as a **bearing instrument with a colour side-job**. Stereo range is a sanity check, not a measurement. The conversation had assumed "camera bearing plus LiDAR range" works out to 15–30 m. It does not. The LD19's 12 m ceiling splits the targeting problem into two regimes. Inside 10 m, two targets resect the station. Beyond 12 m, the choices are three or four targets for bearings-only resection, or GNSS position with the targets used for heading. The 20-minute static-PPK results under canopy make the second option more attractive than expected. That changes what a LiDAR upgrade is for. An S2 or S3 would not improve bearings. What it would buy is range to distant targets, and only on bright targets, since both drop to 10–15 m at 10% reflectivity.

The two largest unknowns cost nothing to resolve. One `lsusb -v` line answers the USB question, and one ChArUco calibration answers the focal-length question, which will also say whether the "29 px" figure or its correction was right. Measure both before buying a lens or writing capture code. After that, the error that matters most is mechanical: the two cameras must hold their relative yaw to within 0.008°.
